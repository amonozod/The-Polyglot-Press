Why the students building CRISPR kits in a converted supply closet might matter more than the lab downtown

A biology elective quietly became the most oversubscribed class on campus. We spent a month with the students trying to explain why gene editing stopped feeling like science fiction.

By Amira Solís, Science Editor · September 8, 2026 · 11 min read

The room used to store cleaning supplies. You can still see the outline where a shelf bolted to the wall, and the paint is two shades lighter in a rectangle nobody has bothered to repaint. Now it holds four secondhand pipettes, a mini-centrifuge bought secondhand from a lab upgrading its equipment, and, on a Tuesday afternoon in March, six students arguing quietly about whether they had pipetted correctly.

This is Introduction to Genetic Engineering, an elective that did not exist two years ago and now has a waiting list longer than the class itself. Ms. Delacroix, who teaches it alongside three sections of standard biology, says she expected a dozen curious students. She got forty-one applications for sixteen seats.

A closet, not a lab

The absence of a real laboratory turns out to matter less than expected, and occasionally more than the school's actual science wing. Students here are not handling human genetic material — the kits use a harmless strain of bacteria and a fluorescent marker gene, the same introductory kit used in several university outreach programs. What they are handling, repeatedly and by hand, is uncertainty: pipetting errors, contaminated samples, a bacterial culture that simply refuses to glow.

"The first two weeks, nothing worked. Nobody's plate glowed. And that was the part where they actually started asking real questions."

That quote belongs to Ms. Delacroix, but it echoes what several students said independently. Kenji Watanabe, a junior who applied for the elective after watching a video essay about CRISPR, described the failure as "the first time science felt like it had stakes I could actually see."

What it's actually training

It would be easy to frame this as a story about early exposure to biotechnology, and it partly is. But the more interesting thread, the one the students kept returning to unprompted, was about patience: the class forces a kind of waiting that most school assignments are designed to avoid. Cultures take 24 hours to grow. Results cannot be rushed, negotiated, or extended for a late slip.

Whether that patience transfers to essays, group projects, or the rest of adolescence is an open question this publication does not have the data to answer. But six students in a converted supply closet seem, for now, convinced that it does.
